# Research Findings: Onboarding Drop-off

*This is a fabricated scenario for training purposes. Quotes and data are illustrative, not real.*

---

## Study overview

**Method:** 45-minute moderated interviews via video call
**Sample:** 8 users who signed up for a free trial in the last 30 days
**Recruitment:** Mix of users who completed onboarding (3) and users who dropped off at the invite step (5)
**Conducted by:** Jordan (Data Analyst) and Priya (Designer)

## Key findings

### Finding 1: Users want to explore alone before committing others

**5 of 8** participants said they wanted to try the tool themselves before involving colleagues. The invite step felt premature.

> "I wanted to kick the tyres first. If I invite my team and it turns out to be rubbish, that's my credibility on the line." — Sarah, P1

> "I'd already been burned by signing my team up for a tool that nobody used. I wasn't doing that again until I was sure." — Rachel, P7

> "I just wanted to see if it could replace our spreadsheets. I didn't need my team for that." — Emma, P5

**Implication:** The current flow asks users to make a social commitment (inviting colleagues) before they've seen enough value to justify it.

### Finding 2: Solo users feel the product isn't for them

**3 of 8** participants were evaluating the tool for personal or solo use. The invite step made them feel like the product wasn't designed for them.

> "When it asked me to invite teammates, I thought — oh, this isn't for freelancers. I nearly closed the tab." — Lisa, P3

> "I use it just for my own project tracking. The whole invite thing felt pushy, like they were trying to get me to sell it to my company." — James, P8

**Implication:** The invite step actively alienates a segment of potential users who could be valuable solo subscribers.

### Finding 3: Users who invited found unexpected value

The **3 completers** all described a moment where collaboration features clicked.

> "Once my team was in, the real-time updates changed everything. That was the moment I thought — yeah, we're switching to this." — Dev, P2

> "I didn't expect the shared dashboard to be that good. That was the thing that made me say, let's go annual." — Marcus, P4

> "The @mention feature in tasks — my team loved it. It replaced about 30 Slack messages a day." — Mark, P6

**Implication:** There's a strong value gap between what users expect (a solo tool) and what they experience (a collaboration platform). The challenge is communicating that value *before* the invite.

### Finding 4: The invite form creates anxiety

**4 of 5** drop-off participants described discomfort with the invite form itself.

> "It wanted me to type in email addresses. That felt very... permanent. Like I was committing to something." — Sarah, P1

> "I didn't have my colleagues' email addresses memorised. I'd need to go look them up, and at that point I just closed it." — Emma, P5

> "What if I invite someone and they get spam? I don't know this company yet." — Rachel, P7

**Implication:** The invite mechanism itself is a barrier. Email entry feels high-commitment and triggers privacy/trust concerns.

### Finding 5: No sense of progress or payoff

**6 of 8** participants couldn't tell how many steps were left or what they'd get at the end.

> "I had no idea how much more I had to do. It felt like one of those forms that just keeps going." — Sarah, P1

> "If it had said 'step 3 of 5' I might have stuck with it. But it felt endless." — Emma, P5

> "I didn't know what I was working towards. What happens when I finish onboarding? A dashboard? A template? I had no picture of what 'done' looked like." — James, P8

**Implication:** Users lack both progress feedback and a compelling vision of the end state.

### Finding 6: Competitors handle this differently

**4 of 8** participants had recently tried competing products.

> "Notion let me just start using it. No invite step, no setup wizard. I brought my team in later because I wanted to, not because I was told to." — James, P8

> "Monday.com showed me a sample project with fake team members. I could see what collaboration looked like before I had to invite anyone." — Rachel, P7

> "Asana let me skip everything and just create a task. I invited my team after a week." — Lisa, P3

**Implication:** Competitors defer the team invite and focus on individual value first. Some use sample/demo content to show collaboration value without requiring real invites.

---

## Researcher notes

- Strongest signal: the gap between "explore alone" intent and the forced invite step. This is the core tension
- Solo users are an underserved segment — they're being pushed away by a flow designed for team buyers
- The 3 completers all described an "aha moment" tied to collaboration features — the value is real, it's just not visible early enough
- Trust is a significant factor — users don't trust an unfamiliar tool with their colleagues' email addresses
- Progress indicators and end-state previews were requested by most participants
